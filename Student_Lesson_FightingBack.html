Chemotherapy: Attacking the Cell Cycle

The oldest cancer drugs target the same cell cycle phases you learned in Lesson 1.

From L3In Lesson 3 you found that Maya was born with one broken TP53 copy, lost the second in one blood cell, and gained an activated NRAS gene. Her leukemia cells have no working p53 brake and a stuck accelerator. Now the question is: how do doctors fight back?
M
Maya's update
"The chemo worked at first. My doctors said the leukemia was gone. Then my checkup eight months later found it had come back."

When leukemia returns after treatment, it's called a relapse. By the end of this lesson you'll understand why Maya's leukemia came back, and what newer treatments could do that chemotherapy couldn't. In class, you'll design her next treatment plan.

📍Start Here

What you'll learn in this lesson

You've spent three lessons learning what goes wrong in a cancer cell. This lesson flips the question: how does each kind of treatment use that biology against the cancer?

1How chemotherapy targets cell cycle phases, and why it can fail
2How targeted therapy blocks a specific broken protein
3How CAR-T immunotherapy turns a patient's immune cells into cancer hunters
4How researchers use shared genomic data and clinical trials to move discoveries from lab to patient
Cancer cells divide constantly, so many chemotherapy drugs attack dividing cells. Each drug strikes at a different point in the cell cycle. Doctors combine several drugs so that cells caught in any phase get hit.
Table 4.1. Three chemotherapy strategies used in childhood ALL
Drug (example)Where it strikesHow it works
🧬MethotrexateS phaseBlocks the cell from making the building blocks of DNA, so it can't copy its chromosomes.
🕸️VincristineM phaseStops spindle fibers from forming, so chromosomes can't be pulled apart. This is the spindle checkpoint from Lesson 1.
💊Steroids (dexamethasone)ApoptosisSignal immature white blood cells (lymphoblasts) to self-destruct.

Why chemo has side effects

Chemo can't tell cancer cells from healthy cells that also divide fast: hair follicles, the lining of the gut, and bone marrow. That's why patients may lose hair, feel sick, and get infections more easily.

Why chemo can stop working

Many chemo drugs work by damaging DNA or jamming division, which signals p53 to trigger apoptosis. A cell with no working p53 may ignore that signal and survive. The survivors keep dividing, and the leukemia comes back.

Why this matters

Combination chemotherapy is why most children with ALL survive today. But it's a one-size-fits-all attack on dividing cells. For children like Maya, whose cancer has a mutation that blunts chemo, doctors need treatments aimed at what's specifically broken, or treatments that don't depend on p53 at all.

Targeted Therapy: Fit the Broken Part

If you know exactly which protein is broken, can you design a drug that blocks only that protein?

A targeted therapy is a drug designed to fit one specific abnormal protein, like a key made for one lock. Because healthy cells don't have that abnormal protein, targeted drugs can hit cancer cells harder while sparing more healthy cells.
🏎 Back to the car analogy

An oncogene is a stuck gas pedal. A targeted drug is like cutting the fuel line to that one pedal: the car stops accelerating, and the rest of the engine keeps working. A missing brake (a lost tumor suppressor) is harder. You can't block a part that isn't there.

A success story: BCR-ABL1. Remember the Philadelphia chromosome from Lesson 3? The fused BCR-ABL1 protein is a signaling enzyme stuck in the "on" position. Drugs called tyrosine kinase inhibitors (such as imatinib) fit into the enzyme's active site and switch it off. When imatinib was added to chemotherapy for children with Philadelphia-chromosome ALL, survival for that group nearly doubled.

Which mutations make good drug targets?

✓ Easier: gain of function

An overactive protein (oncogene) can be blocked. Examples: BCR-ABL1 (very successful), and RAS. RAS was called "undruggable" for decades until the first drug against one form of KRAS was approved in 2021, for lung cancer.

✗ Harder: loss of function

A missing protein (lost tumor suppressor) can't be blocked, because there's nothing to block. Restoring a missing brake like p53 is an active research area, but there is no approved drug that does it yet.

What about Maya's NRAS? There is no approved drug that blocks NRAS directly yet. Researchers are testing drugs that block the next step in the RAS signaling chain (called MEK inhibitors) in clinical trials for children with RAS-mutant leukemia.

CAR-T: Training the Immune System to Hunt

What if the treatment didn't need the cancer cell's p53 at all?

T cells are immune cells that destroy infected or abnormal cells. Leukemia cells often slip past them. CAR-T therapy gives a patient's own T cells a new receptor (a chimeric antigen receptor, or CAR) that locks onto a marker on the leukemia cells.
Why CAR-T can work when chemo fails: A CAR-T cell kills by releasing proteins that directly switch on the leukemia cell's self-destruct machinery. It doesn't need the leukemia cell to sense damage and activate p53. So a leukemia cell with no working TP53 can still be destroyed, as long as it carries CD19.

The evidence

In a global clinical trial, 61 of 75 children and young adults (81%) whose B-cell ALL had relapsed or resisted treatment went into remission after a single CAR-T infusion. In 2017 this became the first CAR-T therapy approved by the U.S. FDA.

The trade-offs

  • Cytokine release syndrome: a strong immune reaction (fever, low blood pressure) in about 3 of 4 trial patients. Usually treatable.
  • CAR-T cells also destroy healthy B cells, so patients may need antibody infusions to help fight infections.

Why this matters

CAR-T is a direct application of what you know: which cell type the cancer came from (B cell) decides which surface marker can be targeted, and which gene is broken (TP53) helps explain why older treatments failed. One question to carry into class: would CD19 CAR-T work for every kind of leukemia?

Genomic Data Portals: Thousands of Patients, One Question

One child's genetic report helps one child. Thousands of reports, shared safely, can help every child.

After tumor–normal sequencing, many families agree to let their child's genetic data be shared for research, with names and identifying details removed. Researchers combine these data into genomic data portals: searchable databases that anyone can use to ask questions across thousands of patients.

🔍 Find patterns

Which genes are mutated most often in each type of childhood cancer?

🎯 Find targets

How many patients have a mutation that an existing drug can block?

👥 Find "patients like mine"

How did other children with the same mutations respond to treatment?

Privacy and consent: Data in research portals are de-identified (no names, addresses, or birthdays) and shared only with family permission. Access to the most detailed data usually requires researchers to apply and agree to strict rules.
Real portals you can look at: The U.S. National Cancer Institute's Childhood Cancer Data Initiative and the open-source cBioPortal both host pediatric cancer genomic data. Several children's cancer research hospitals also run their own public data portals.

From Lab to Patient

How a discovery becomes a treatment, and how that journey changed childhood leukemia.

A new treatment idea starts in the lab and must pass through clinical trials (carefully monitored studies with patients who volunteer) before doctors can use it routinely.
Table 4.2. The path from discovery to treatment
StageMain question
🔬 Lab researchDoes the idea work in cells and animal models? (For example, do CAR-T cells kill CD19 cells in a dish?)
Phase 1 trialIs it safe? What dose is right? (Small group of patients)
Phase 2 trialDoes it work for this cancer? (A larger group, like the 75-patient CAR-T trial)
Phase 3 trialIs it better than the current best treatment? (Often hundreds of patients, compared head to head)
✅ ApprovalRegulators review all the evidence. Doctors keep tracking long-term effects.
What clinical trials did for childhood ALL: In the early 1960s, childhood ALL was almost always fatal. By the late 1960s, about 1 in 10 children were cured. In a study of more than 21,000 children treated in clinical trials, 5-year survival rose from 83.7% (1990–1994) to 90.4% (2000–2005). Most of that gain came from using existing drugs more wisely, one trial at a time.
