Two Kinds of Cancer Genes, Up Close

You know the gas pedal and the brake. Now let's look at how each one actually breaks.

From L2 In Lesson 2 you learned that oncogenes act like a stuck gas pedal, tumor suppressors act like brakes, and Maya's leukemia cells had lost both copies of the TP53 brake gene and carried an activated RAS gas-pedal gene. Lesson 3 asks a deeper question: where did those mutations come from, and what does that mean for Maya's family?
M
Maya's mom has a question
"Did Maya get this from us? Her little brother Eli is eight. Should we be worried about him too?"

The care team can't answer that with a guess. They need genetic evidence. By the end of this lesson, you'll read Maya's genetic report and make an evidence-based claim that answers her mom's question.

A proto-oncogene is a normal, healthy gene that tells a cell to grow and divide when it's supposed to. It only becomes a dangerous oncogene when a mutation leaves it stuck in the "on" position. There are three main ways that happens.
Table 1. Three ways a proto-oncogene becomes an oncogene
How it happensWhat changesReal example
✏️Point mutation A single DNA base is changed. The protein's shape changes so it can't switch itself off. RAS genes. One letter change locks the RAS protein in its "divide" setting. RAS mutations are found in many childhood leukemias, including Maya's.
📑Gene amplification The cell makes many extra copies of the gene. The protein is normal, but there is far too much of it. MYCN in neuroblastoma, a nerve-tissue cancer of young children. Tumors with many MYCN copies tend to grow more aggressively.
🔀Chromosomal translocation Pieces of two different chromosomes break and swap places, fusing two genes into one hybrid gene that is always on. BCR-ABL1 (the "Philadelphia chromosome"), found in some cases of ALL. The fused protein sends a nonstop growth signal.

Why one hit is enough for an oncogene, but not for a tumor suppressor

Oncogene = Dominant at the cell level

You have two copies of every gene. If one copy of a proto-oncogene becomes stuck "on," it keeps sending growth signals no matter what the normal copy does. One broken copy is enough. That's a gain of function.

Tumor suppressor = Recessive at the cell level

If one copy of a tumor suppressor breaks, the other copy can still make enough brake protein. The cell only loses control when both copies are lost. That's a loss of function, and it's why tumor suppressors need two hits.

Why this matters

Knowing how an oncogene was switched on tells doctors what to aim at. The BCR-ABL1 fusion protein, for example, can be blocked by a targeted drug that fits into the hybrid protein like a key jammed into a lock. Children with this kind of ALL now get that drug alongside chemotherapy. You'll design treatments like this yourself in Lesson 4.

The Two-Hit Hypothesis: Weighing the Evidence

A hypothesis is only as strong as the evidence behind it. Let's look at the data that convinced scientists.

In 1971, a physician-scientist named Alfred Knudson studied children with retinoblastoma, a rare cancer of the retina that usually appears before age 5. Some of these children had a parent who'd had the same cancer. Most did not. Knudson compared the two groups and noticed a pattern.
Comparison of hereditary and non-hereditary retinoblastoma
ObservationHereditary casesNon-hereditary cases
Share of all casesAbout 4 in 10About 6 in 10
Eyes affectedUsually both eyesUsually one eye
Number of tumorsOften severalUsually one
Typical age at diagnosisEarlier, often in the first yearLater, often around age 2
Family historyOften presentRarely present

Patterns described by Knudson (1971) and later retinoblastoma studies. Values are rounded to show the pattern.

Knudson's reasoning: Suppose a child inherits one broken copy of a "retinoblastoma gene" in every cell. Then any single retinal cell needs just one more hit to lose both copies. With millions of retinal cells, that's likely to happen early, more than once, and in both eyes. A child born with two working copies needs two hits in the same cell, which is much rarer. That would explain why those tumors show up later, and usually only once.
The prediction came true. Knudson's idea was a prediction: there should be a single gene whose two copies both have to be lost. Fifteen years later, in 1986, researchers found it: RB1, the first tumor suppressor gene ever identified. A prediction confirmed by later, independent evidence is one of the strongest kinds of support a hypothesis can get.
🤔 Think about it
Wait. If tumor suppressors are recessive (both copies must break), why does hereditary retinoblastoma run in families like a dominant trait, showing up in about half the children of an affected parent?
What's inherited is one broken copy, which passes to each child with a 50% chance, just like any dominant trait. But a child who inherits it has millions of retinal cells, each one only a single hit away from losing both copies. A second hit in at least one of those cells is almost certain, so about 9 in 10 carriers develop retinoblastoma. The risk is inherited in a dominant pattern, while the gene still acts recessively inside each cell.

💡
Why this matters

Knudson's logic is still how researchers spot inherited cancer risk today. When cancers appear unusually early, in more than one place, or in several relatives, it's a signal that a first hit may have been inherited. That's exactly the pattern doctors look for in Li-Fraumeni syndrome, the inherited TP53 condition you met in Lesson 2.

Inherited or Acquired? Germline vs. Somatic

Where a mutation happens decides who it affects: one person's tumor, or a whole family.

Every mutation happens in a cell. The key question is which cell. A mutation in an egg or sperm ends up in every cell of the child who develops from it. A mutation in one body cell later in life stays in that cell and the cells it divides into.

🧬 Germline mutation

  • Present in the egg or sperm, so it's there from conception
  • Found in every cell of the body
  • Can be passed to children (50% chance per child)
  • Shows up in normal tissue like skin, saliva, or blood

🔬 Somatic mutation

  • Happens in one body cell after conception
  • Found only in that cell and its descendants, such as the tumor
  • Cannot be passed to children
  • Shows up in the tumor but not in normal tissue

How doctors tell the difference

Tumor–normal sequencing. Doctors read the DNA from two samples: the tumor, and a sample of normal tissue from the same patient. A mutation found in both samples was there from the start, so it's germline. A mutation found only in the tumor was acquired, so it's somatic. For leukemia, the normal sample is usually skin or saliva, not blood, because blood is full of leukemia cells.
How common are inherited cancer genes in children? A large study that sequenced the genomes of more than 1,000 children with cancer (2015) found that roughly 8 to 9 out of every 100 carried a germline mutation in a cancer-risk gene. So for most children with cancer, the mutations that drove it were somatic. But for some, a first hit was inherited, and finding it changes care for the whole family.
🤔 Present at birth ≠ inherited
Some childhood leukemia mutations have been found in blood saved from a child's newborn screening test, years before the leukemia appeared. Does that mean they were inherited?
Not necessarily. Those mutations arose in one developing blood cell while the baby was growing before birth. They were somatic: not in the parents, not in every cell, and not passable to the child's own future children. They were simply an early first hit. A later second hit was still needed before leukemia developed. "When" a mutation happened and "whether it was inherited" are two different questions.

💡
Why this matters

This one distinction decides whether a diagnosis stays with one patient or becomes a question for the whole family. When a germline mutation is found, a genetic counselor can offer testing to parents and siblings, and relatives who carry it can start regular screening to catch any cancer early, when it's most treatable.

Caretakers: When DNA Repair Breaks Down

Some genes don't control division at all. They keep the DNA clean, and losing them speeds everything up.

Your DNA is damaged thousands of times a day in every cell, by copying mistakes, chemicals, and radiation such as UV light. Almost all of it gets fixed. That's the job of a team of DNA repair genes.
Gatekeepers

Control division directly

Genes like RB1 and TP53 decide whether a cell may pass a checkpoint. Losing them lets a damaged cell divide.

Caretakers

Keep the DNA accurate

DNA repair genes find and fix damage. Losing them doesn't cause division on its own. It makes new mutations pile up faster, so gatekeepers get hit sooner.

Table 2. Three DNA repair systems and the genes that run them
Repair systemWhat it fixesKey genes
Mismatch repairCopying mistakes made during S phase. Think of it as spell-check for DNA replication.MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2
Homologous recombinationBreaks through both strands of the DNA double helix, repaired using the matching chromosome as a templateBRCA1, BRCA2
Nucleotide excision repairBulky damage, such as kinks caused by UV light from the sunXP genes (XPA, XPC, and others)

Simplified model for illustration. Real mutation rates are far smaller per division, but the gap between working and broken repair is just as dramatic. You'll explore this in depth in class with the DNA Repair Simulator.
A pediatric example: In a rare condition called constitutional mismatch repair deficiency (CMMRD), a child inherits a broken mismatch repair gene from both parents, so no cell in their body has working mismatch repair. These children have a very high risk of developing brain tumors, leukemia, or lymphoma during childhood, which is why they need frequent screening from a young age.

Key Vocabulary Review

Gene amplification
Extra copies of a gene, producing too much of a normal protein
Chromosomal translocation
Two chromosomes swap pieces, which can fuse two genes into an always-on hybrid
Germline mutation
A mutation present from conception in every cell; can be inherited and passed on
Somatic mutation
A mutation acquired in one body cell after conception; found only in that cell's descendants
Caretaker gene
A DNA repair gene that keeps the mutation rate low
Tumor–normal sequencing
Comparing DNA from a tumor and from normal tissue to sort germline from somatic mutations

💡
Why this matters

Here's a twist: tumors with broken mismatch repair carry so many mutations that they start to look "foreign" to the immune system. That makes them good candidates for immunotherapy, treatments that help the patient's own immune cells recognize and attack the cancer. A weakness in the cancer's DNA becomes a target. You'll see more of this in Lesson 4.

Make Your Claim: Maya's Genetic Report

Time to answer Maya's mom. Read the evidence, then make and defend a claim.

Maya's care team ran tumor–normal sequencing. They compared DNA from her leukemia cells (bone marrow) with DNA from a small skin sample. Her leukemia has fewer chromosomes than normal, a subtype called low-hypodiploid ALL. TP53 mutations are common in this subtype, and in roughly 4 out of 10 of those children, the TP53 mutation turns out to be inherited.

Model CER

Claim: Maya's first TP53 hit was inherited (germline), and her second TP53 hit and her NRAS mutation were acquired (somatic).

Evidence: The TP53 copy-1 mutation was detected in both her leukemia cells and her normal skin cells. The TP53 copy-2 deletion and the NRAS mutation were found only in her leukemia cells. Her family history includes two relatives on her mother's side with cancer at unusually young ages (breast cancer at 34, osteosarcoma at 15).

Reasoning: Skin cells aren't cancer cells, so a mutation in skin must have been present in every cell from conception, which makes it germline. That means Maya was born with one hit, like the hereditary retinoblastoma children. Only one more hit (the copy-2 deletion) was needed in a single blood cell to lose the TP53 brake completely. The early cancers in her mother's family fit the pattern of an inherited TP53 mutation (Li-Fraumeni syndrome), though only testing her parents could confirm which parent carries it.

💡
Why this matters

What you just did is what a tumor board and a genetic counselor do for real families. Reading evidence carefully, separating what the data shows from what it only suggests, and explaining it clearly to a worried parent are core skills in cancer care, and they all come from understanding the biology you've built across three lessons.
