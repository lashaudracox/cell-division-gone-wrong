Family Genetics Consult: The Maya Case

Four specialists, one family, one big question: should Eli be tested?

🏥 Real-world connection: When a child's cancer might be inherited, children's cancer research hospitals bring in a genetic counselor alongside the oncology team. Together they review the lab results and family history, then help the family decide about testing for relatives. Today your group is that team.
📁 The Evidence: Review Together Before You Split Into Roles
Clue 1 · Diagnosis
Maya, age 12: B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL), low-hypodiploid subtype. Her leukemia cells have fewer chromosomes than normal. TP53 mutations are common in this subtype, and in roughly 4 in 10 of these children the TP53 mutation is inherited.

👥 Round 1 · Your Role: Answer Individually, Then Share

Read your role card and answer its questions on your own (about 8 minutes). Then take turns sharing with your team.

🧬 Genetic CounselorFamily history & inheritance

You read the family tree and explain inheritance to the family.

  1. Circle every relative with cancer on the pedigree. Which side of the family are they on? What is unusual about their ages?
  2. If Maya's first TP53 hit is germline, it came from one parent. Using the pedigree, which parent is more likely, and why?
  3. If that parent carries it, what is the chance that Eli inherited it? Show your reasoning.

🩺 Pediatric OncologistPatient care

You decide how this genetic finding affects care for Maya and her family.

  1. An inherited TP53 mutation means Maya's other cells are each one hit away from losing TP53. Why might doctors try to limit radiation (like CT scans and radiation therapy) when treating her?
  2. Children who carry a germline TP53 mutation are offered regular screening, often including whole-body MRI. Why start screening in childhood instead of waiting until adulthood?
  3. Does knowing her first hit was inherited change which mutations are in her leukemia cells? Explain.
